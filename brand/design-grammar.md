# Crown and Compass — Design Grammar

**How a Crown and Compass design gets made.** This is the working spec for
apparel, coins, prints and any other object that carries the mark. Read it
before generating anything.

Companion to `voice.md` (how we write) and `brand-standards.md` (the identity).
Voice governs sentences. This governs objects.

---

## The problem this solves

Most Christian merchandise fails the same way: a centered illustration with a
phrase floating underneath it. Nothing holds the composition, so it reads as a
picture printed on a shirt rather than as an emblem — a thing with authority,
like a seal, a coin, or a regimental crest.

Everything below exists to prevent that.

---

## The thread — never varies

These five hold across every design we make. They are what make a stranger
recognise two different pieces as ours.

1. **Engraved line technique.** Build value with line, not tone — tapered
   strokes, parallel hatching for planes, stipple for soft falloff. Density of
   line equals darkness. No painted rendering, no photographic source, no
   digital gradient meshes.

2. **Distress is subtractive.** Age is knocked *out* of solid ink with an
   eroded-plate mask. It is never a grey wash laid on top. This is the single
   most important technical rule here: subtractive distress keeps hard edges and
   real contrast, while additive grunge goes soft and muddy — and soft and muddy
   is what looks cheap.

3. **Bone, never pure white.** `#F1EBDC` is the base ink. Pure white reads
   clinical; bone reads aged. One accent per design, doing exactly one job.

4. **Two registers of type, hard-separated.** The statement and its source are
   never the same face at the same size. Display line in a slab serif; citation
   in small letterspaced monospace capitals, set as a footnote. A citation makes
   a design humble — it says *I did not make this up, and you can check.*

5. **Pre-1700 object vocabulary.** Anchors, blades, crowns, books, clasps,
   chains, lamps, rope, stone, wheat, ships, instruments. One modern object
   breaks the whole system.

**And one prohibition:** we do not depict Christ's face, or any figure standing
in for him. The lamb, the lion, the crown, the thorns, the empty throne carry
him instead. Substitution keeps the work symbolic; literal depiction is where
this genre tips into kitsch.

## The rotation — must vary

**A design system that outputs one shape forever is a template, not a system.**
The variety is deliberate: different pieces should look like the work of
different hands that happen to share a discipline. Rotate all of these.

### Composition archetype — never twice in a row

| Archetype | What it is | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Seal** | Circular, type on a ring around an engraved centrepiece, milled rim | The mark itself; anything that must also become a coin |
| **Architectural badge** | Arch, shield or diamond frame with a double hairline; type follows the frame's own geometry | Grave, formal statements |
| **Sandwich** | Display line above, illustration, display line below, citation beneath | A single object with a short line |
| **Placard** | Heavy condensed capitals in a hard rectangle. **No illustration at all** | Blunt, declarative, deadpan |
| **Back panel** | One large composition at shoulder-blade scale, reduced to a small mark on the front | Scenes, drama, more colour |

### Also rotate

- **Accent colour** — ember `#C0552A`, antique gold `#C9A227`, oxblood
  `#8B2E1F`, or none.
- **Register** — grave, defiant, still, dramatic, plain.
- **Colour count** — most designs are two or three inks. A full-scene back panel
  may run five. Occasional deliberate exceptions keep the catalogue alive.
- **Scale** — small chest mark or full back. **Never a timid mid-size graphic:**
  that is the most reliable tell of cheap merchandise.

## Choosing the text

### The three themes, rotating

- **Knowledge** — books, study, the word, wisdom, what ignorance costs.
- **King Jesus** — crown, throne, sovereignty, authority, what is owed.
- **Navigation** — path, bearing, anchor, lamp, harbour, true north.

### The hard filter: concrete imagery

**A verse qualifies only if it contains objects you can draw.** This is the
selection rule that matters most, and it disqualifies most well-loved verses.

- *Unusable:* "Trust in the Lord with all your heart." True, and it draws
  nothing.
- *Usable:* "sharper than any two-edged sword", "an anchor of the soul", "ask
  for the ancient paths", "they cast their crowns before him".

If the verse gives you no object, it is not a design. Move on.

### The argument test

**State the meaning as one object doing something to another.** A skull
*resting on* a locked book. A sword *laid across* an open book, cutting the
pages. An anchor *driven into* fractured rock. Crowns *cast down* on a step.

If you cannot phrase it that way, the design is decoration and will look like
it. The relationship between the objects *is* the sentence — that is why a
still life beats a landscape or a diagram: the idea arrives instantly, with no
interpretive work.

**Two conditions on that sentence, and they do the real work.**

*The objects must be touching.* One object in a setting is not two objects. If
the second thing in your sentence is the ground, a field, a room, a horizon or
the sky, you have written a scene and the argument has quietly gone missing.

*The verb must be one you can see at 44mm.* Driven, split, cut, cast down,
pinned, crushed, laid across — a strike, not a state. A key *turning* in a lock
and a furrow *bending* around a stone both pass the sentence test on paper and
fail on the plate, because the action lives in a detail that disappears the
moment the design shrinks. Write the verb, then ask whether a stranger across a
room could name it.

The placard is the one archetype these two conditions cannot govern, because it
carries no illustration. There the force has to live in the sentence itself, and
the verse still has to survive the concrete-imagery filter above.

Corollary: a scene makes the viewer hunt for the point, and a diagram hides it
in a detail. Both also fail at coin scale.

## Naming

The design's name is **not** the text on the garment. It is a one-to-four word
fragment — a bare noun or verb — that acts as a handle so one idea can travel
across shirt, coin, mug and print, be searched for, and be redrawn later.

*Lack. Sharper. Anchor. Cast Down. Holds.*

Never a sentence. Never a pun.

---

## Design for the reduction

Complexity sheds as the surface shrinks: full composition on a shirt → simplified
mark on a decal → single emblem on a hat patch or a 44mm coin → emblem only on a
mug.

**Seals survive that reduction. Scenes do not.** If an idea has to run across
every format — and the challenge coins do — build it as a seal from the start.
That is a constraint to design *to*, not around.

---

## What a finished concept contains

1. **Handle** — the name, 1–4 words
2. **Theme** — Knowledge, King Jesus, or Navigation
3. **Scripture** — book, chapter, verse
4. **Argument** — one sentence: X doing something to Y
5. **Archetype** — from the rotation table
6. **Palette** — ground, base ink, accent
7. **Image prompt** — with the text spelled out exactly and its placement named
8. **Type spec** — which face, which register, on which path
9. **Label** — two or three sentences explaining the iconography, in `voice.md`
   voice. This is opt-in copy: it lives below the fold, never on the garment.

---

## Reject before generating

- Any object later than 1700
- Christ's face, or a figure standing in for him
- More than one accent colour outside a deliberate full-scene exception
- The same archetype as the previous design
- An emblem intended for a coin that will not read at 44mm
- A verse with no drawable object
- A design whose argument cannot be stated as X-doing-something-to-Y
- Two objects that never touch, or a verb that vanishes at 44mm
- A horizon, a ground plane, a room or a field — objects sit on the plate, not
  in a place
- Pure white ink
- A mid-size chest graphic

## Worked examples

Three that landed, deliberately unalike:

**STUDY** · Knowledge · 2 Timothy 2:15 · **Placard**
Four lines of heavy condensed capitals in a hard keyline box, no illustration at
all, one word in oxblood. Flat, deadpan, no distress. Reads like a printer's
proof.

**DESIRED HAVEN** · Navigation · Psalm 107:29 · **Back panel**
A ship heeled over in a heavy sea, the water flat and glassy in a circle around
the hull while the storm rages beyond. Five colours. Maritime engraving.

**KING OF KINGS** · King Jesus · Revelation 19:16 · **Seal**
A crown in deep engraved relief inside a milled coin rim, gold-dominant, one
oxblood jewel. Struck metal rather than printed ink — this one *is* the
challenge coin.

Same hand. Three different shapes. That is the target.

## A note on writing prompts for image generators

Spell the text out exactly, in quotes, and say where it sits. Name the colours
by hex. Describe the physical relationship between the objects, not just the
objects.

Expect curved type to be the weak point — text on an arc is the hardest case for
any generator. If the artwork is right and the lettering is mangled, generate the
art alone and set the type in vector afterwards. Zilla Slab and IBM Plex Mono
map onto the display and citation registers.

# Part Two — The Photographic Register

Everything above governs **emblems**: merch, coins, prints. Engraved line,
bone ink, pre-1700 objects.

**The site's photography is a different system, and the two must not be
confused.** An engraved seal on a blog post would look like clip art. A
photograph on a coin is impossible. One rule carries across, and it is the
important one — *objects carry the argument*. Everything else differs.

## Where these images appear

Blog hero images and card thumbnails, set as a filled background behind a dark
overlay. Note that until a real photograph exists, `.imgph::after` renders the
`data-label` on screen as `IMAGE · <label>`. **So the label is visible UI, not
a private note.** Write it as a sentence a reader could see, because sometimes
they will.

## The five rules

**1. One light source, warm and small.** A lamp. A lantern on a porch rail. A
phone glowing face down. Low lamplight on a table. Golden hour on a hillside.
Never overhead daylight, never a flash, never an evenly lit room. The single
light is what makes a photograph feel like a moment somebody is actually
living in rather than a stock image of a concept.

**2. Dusk, evening, or night.** Almost without exception. The brightest thing
in the catalogue is golden hour on a dry trail. Darkness is doing real work
here: these posts are read by men at the end of a day, and the images should
look like the hour they are being read in.

**3. People are implied, seen from behind, or absent.** Four men around a table
photographed from behind. One figure walking a ridge. An empty porch with the
family out. **No faces, no eye contact, no models.** A face makes the reader
watch someone else; an empty chair makes him take the seat.

**4. Everything is worn.** Worn wood, a cluttered desk, dusty boots, a stack of
used books, an unfinished manuscript. Nothing new, nothing styled, nothing
staged. Wear is what says a real man has been here.

**5. One object carries the argument.** This is the rule shared with the
emblems, and it is the difference between a photograph and decoration. A
wedding ring set down on a table. A phone face down in a dark room. The object
states the post's tension before a word is read.

## What breaks it

- Faces, eye contact, models, anything that reads as stock photography
- Daylight, overhead lighting, bright evenly lit rooms
- New, clean, styled or staged objects
- Literal illustration of the title — a post about sedation does not want a
  photograph of a drink. It wants the single lit window in a dark street.
- Text baked into the image (the page sets its own type)
- Engraved or illustrated treatments — those belong to Part One

## Writing an image prompt

Name the object, the light, the hour, and the wear. Ten to twenty words is
usually enough, and the existing labels are the model:

> a phone glowing in a dark room, face down on a table
> four men around a table at night, seen from behind, warm lamplight
> a wedding ring resting on a worn wooden table
> an unfinished manuscript on a cluttered desk

For a generator, extend that with the register: photographic, natural light,
shallow depth of field, muted and warm, film grain, no text, no faces.

**Write the prompt from the post's argument, not its title.** The post about
men numbing themselves wants a single lit window in a dark street with every
other house asleep — the isolation the piece is actually about. A photograph
of a glass on a bar would illustrate the subject and miss the point.
